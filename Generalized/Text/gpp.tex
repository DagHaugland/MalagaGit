\section{Introduction}
\label{intro}

Ever since its discovery \cite{haverly78}, the \textsc{Pooling Problem} has been recognized as a computational challenge.
Despite resemblence with well-solved network flow problems, like the \textsc{Minimum Cost Flow Problem} and the \textsc{Maximum Flow Problem},
presence of constraints on the flow composition disables any linear programming (LP) representation of the problem.
While the flow composition at the network sources is part of the input data, it depends on the flow values at other network nodes.
At the sinks, bounds on the proportions of key components apply,
reflecting that customers impose constraints not only on the quantity delivered, but also on the quality.
Flow streams merging at any node are blended linearly,
producing a mix where the concentration of each component is a weighted mean of entering concentrations.
Unless the network structure satisfies rather strict conditions, such as absence of intermediate nodes (pools) between sources and sinks,
the resulting problem is NP-hard and unlikely to be LP-representable.

\subsection{Problem variants and model formulations} \label{sec:form}

The \textsc{Pooling Problem} literature distinguishes the \textsc{Standard Pooling Problem} (SPP)
from the \textsc{Generalized Pooling Problem} (GPP).
In the standard variant, no paths from sources to sinks in the network consist of more than three nodes.
This means that a three-layered structure is assumed, where the node set is partitioned into sources, pools, and sinks.
Only sources can be upstream neighbors of pools, and only sinks can be downstream neighbors.
In contrast, the generalized variant also allows pools to be adjacent to other pools, and flow paths may have arbitrary length. 
Despite its considerably more restrictive definition, even the \textsc{Standard Pooling Problem} is NP-hard \cite{alfaki13a}.
Hardness persists for instances with only one pool, and instances with only two sources and sinks \cite{haugland16a},
whereas even the generalized variant is LP-representable in instances with only one source or one sink.
For more detailed analyses of the boundary between tracktable and intractable instance classes,
the reader is referred to \cite{haugland16b,boland17,baltean18}.

Most mathematical models of the \textsc{Pooling Problem} are built upon two or more sets of variables.
The (single-commodity) \emph{flow variables} are considered as independent variables,
follow the pattern from traditional network flow models, like, e.g., the \textsc{Minimum Cost Flow Problem}.
In order to track the flow composition on paths from sources to sinks, \emph{auxiliary variables} are required.
In the earliest formulations emerging in the literature \cite{haverly78,baker85},
each auxiliary variable represents the concentration of some flow component.
For instance, in oil industry applications where the delivered products are subject to upper bounds on the relative CO2-content,
a variable for the CO2 concentration is introduced for each network pool.
Thus, the auxiliary variables are considered to be \emph{dependent},
in the sense that their values are uniquely given by the flow variable values.
Models with flow and concentration variables inevitably involve products across the two sets,
particularly in material balance equations for every component at every pool.
Consequently, the \textsc{Pooling Problem} is frequently referred to as a \emph{bilinear} problem.

The auxiliary variables may alternatively represent \emph{source proportions}.
Each such variable is associated with a source-node pair,
and determines the proportion of the flow through the node that originates from the source.
Such formulations are interesting from a computational viewpoint, although bilinear constraints are still necessary.
The same applies if the source proportion variables are replaced or extended by sink proportions, 
which, analogously, correspond to the relative amount of the flow at a node that in the end is delivered at a given sink.
Regardless of how the auxilary variables are chosen,
their necessary presence is the main reason for the hardness of the \textsc{Pooling Problem}.

A bilinear model combining flow and concentration variables is referred to as the P-formulation \cite{haverly78} of the
standard \textsc{Pooling Problem}.
Replacemant of concentration by source proportion variables leads to the Q-formulation \cite{bental94},
which after addition of redundant valid inequalities gives the PQ-formulation \cite{sahinidis05}.
These early contributions motivated more research into model formulations,
aiming for strong dual bounds provided by the corresponding linear relaxations.
Analogous to the PQ-formulation, sink proportions replace the source proportions to produce the TP-formulation \cite{alfaki13a}.
Depending on the instance, the linear relaxation may give stronger bounds when the model contains variables for both source and sink proportions,
which is referred to as the STP-formulation \cite{alfaki13a}.

Extension to the generalized \textsc{Pooling Problem} is straightforward in the case of the P-formulation.
The Q- and PQ-formulations are extended in \cite{alfaki13b}, while the extensions of the TP- and STP-formulations 
are covered in \citep{boland16}.
Proportion variables corresponding to pairs consisting of a source and a sink can give even stronger formulations.
\citet{boland16} formulate the generalized \textsc{Pooling Problem} in terms of such variables,
which for each pool expresses the relative amount of the flow that is supplied from the given source and delivered to the sink.
Whereas such a formulation is proved to be at least as strong as the aforementioned models,
the large number of variables incurs a high computational cost.
For each pool, one proportion variable for each source-sink pair is associated,
implying that the worst-case growth in the number of auxiliary variables is a cubic function of the node cardinality.

\subsection{Relaxations and solution algorithms} \label{sec:relax}

Linear relaxations of bilinear optimization models are achieved through replacement of each bilinear term by a new variable,
which is constrained by the so-called McCormick inequalities \cite{mccormick76}.
It is well known \cite{mccormick76} that with two variables constrained within a rectangular set,
the convex (concave) envelope of a bilinear function is given as the maximum (minimum) of two affine functions.
McCormick relaxations have been exploited in numerous algorithms for general bilinear programs since they started to
emerge in the eighties \cite{alkhayyal83,alkhayyal90}.
Incorporated in a tree-search (branch-and-bound, branch-and-cut) scheme, where branching involves refinement of one or more rectangular regions,
the relaxation provides a dual bound that in the limit collapses with the primal bound corresponding to the best feasible solution found.
Computational studies of \textsc{Pooling Problem} algorithms often cover approaches built upon this idea
\cite{foulds92,bental94,quesada95,audet04,misener09,misener10,misener11,alfaki13a,alfaki13b,boland16},
showing how their efficiency depends on, e.g., the strength of the formulation chosen. 
Also available software for bilinear programs in general \cite{tawarmalani04,gurobi26}
or the \textsc{Pooling Problem} in particular \cite{misener11,ceccon22} follow a tree-search algorithm
solving linear or piecewise linear subproblems.

Aiming for faster convergence of the algorithms, several attempts to strengthen the relaxations have been made.
One approach is to subdivide the range of the flow variables into smaller sections,
and add the McCormick inequalities corresponding to each section \cite{meyer06,wicaksono08,gounaris09,pham09,misener11,dey15,gupte17}.
This results in a \emph{piecewise} linear relaxation,
which requires binary variables in order to ensure consistency between
variable values and the inequalities that apply in the corresponding section.
By recognizing the complicating constraint functions as rank-one products,
and observing that the convex hull of such functions under mild assumptions is second-order-cone representable,
stronger relaxations are obtained \cite{dey20,jalilian26}.

Alternatively, the convex and concave envelopes can be defined over non-rectangular regions.
In the case of a bilinear function on a \emph{triangular} 
domain, \citet{linderoth05} shows that the envelopes are given in terms of convex nonlinear inequalities,
providing stronger relaxations than the traditional McCormick relaxations.
Allowing convex nonlinear inequalities, \citet{luedtke20} not only strengthen the relaxation,
but also achieve a convex hull formulation in a restricted set of instances.

Decomposition approaches were among the first methods targeting exact solutions to the \textsc{Pooling Problem}.
This includes, e.g., Benders decomposition \cite{floudas90a,visweswaran90}, and various methods based on Lagrangean duality
\cite{adhya99,floudas90b}.
Since then, tree-search methods seem to receive the majority of the attention from the research community.

Local optimization methods, in particular successive linear programming \cite{haverly78,baker85}  dominated
the \textsc{Pooling Problem} research before the advent of the first exact methods \cite{floudas90a,floudas90b}.
In large instances, such methods still have considerable merit \cite{grothey23}.
As exact solvers have become able to solve larger problem instances,
the role of local methods is to provide fairly good solutions when optimality is not provable in practical time.
To that end, several approaches are available.
Noteworthy among metaheuristics is the variable-neighborhood-search method by \citet{audet04}.
Augmenting-flow methods \cite{alfaki14,xiang26} optimize the flow to a single sink in each iteration.
When the flow paths to the sink are not allowed to intersect pools that already are assigned positive flow,
the subproblems become linear in each iteration, since the single-sink \textsc{Pooling Problem} is LP-representable \cite{haugland16a}.
Allowing such intersections imply bilinear subproblems with modest computational cost due to few bilinear terms.

\subsection{Discretization and anticipated sparse solutions} \label{sec:sparse}

Despite recent progress in both exact solvers and local methods, experimental studies show that beyond instances of moderate size,
there is a considerable gap between dual bounds provided by linear relaxations and the best solutions computed by heuristics.
The purpose of the current paper is to narrow down this gap.
Because bilinearity is the main source of computational challenge, the key idea is to restrict the search to feasible subregions
that can be modeled without bilinear functions.
\citet{baltean18} argue that optimal solutions to \textsc{Pooling Problem} instances tend to be \emph{sparse}.
This motivates a search for solutions with a sparsity pattern that can be modeled by means of linear inequalities and binary variables.

Generalizing \cite[Proposition 3]{haugland16a}, it is observed that the \textsc{Pooling Problem} is an LP in instances where
every pool is connected to only one source or to only one sink.
Consequently, restricting the \emph{support} of the flow, i.e., the subgraph induced by flow-carrying arcs, to have this structure,
reduces the \textsc{Pooling Problem} to a mixed integer \emph{linear} program.
The integer (binary) variables correspond to the selection of the source or sink to constitute the unique connection to a pool.
Given the maturity of available MILP solvers, especially when compared with their bilinear programming counterparts,
it is reasonable to believe that confining the search to MILP-representable parts of the feasible region will result in a considerable speed-up.

Restricting the support as suggested implies a flow graph with a particular \emph{sparsity pattern}:
Any two paths, starting at distinct sources and ending at distinct sinks, are also pool-disjoint.
Equivalently, at any pool, all flow originates from a unique source, or all the flow is delivered to a unique sink.
Recently, the \textsc{Minimum Cost Flow Problem} subject to these conditions, referred to as extended \emph{confluent flow} conditions,
was studied \cite{haugland26}.
Partly by pursuing ideas from \cite{haugland26}, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item A hardness proof for a variant of the \textsc{Pooling Problem} with fixed demand \cite{ruiz13,grothey23} is provided.
\item MILP models are derived for sparse solutions to the generalized \textsc{Pooling Problem}.
\item Redundant valid inequalities for the MILP models are given.
\item A comprehensive computational study is made, comparing the new models with a state-of-the-art bilinear solver.
Most challenging instances available from the literature, in addition to new instances generated as part of this study,
are included in the experiments.
As an outcome of this, improvements over the best known solutions and the best known bounds are made in several instances.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows:
Following some basic definitions, Section~\ref{sec:notation} defines the problem and reviews mathematical formulations of the
\textsc{Pooling Problem} from the literature.
The section also gives new complexity results for a problem variant with fixed demand.
Section~\ref{sec:conf} reviews the concept of confluent network flow, along with its extensions.
These concepts are exploted to formulate MILP models for sparse inner-approximations of the generalized \textsc{Pooling Problem}.
Finally, computational experiemnts are reported in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\section{Mathematical notation and problem formulation}\label{sec:notation}

Let $D=(N,A)$ be a directed graph,
where the node set $N=S\cup P\cup T$ consists of the disjoint subsets $S$ of \emph{sources}, $P$ of \emph{pools}, and $T$ of \emph{sinks}.
It is assumed that $A\subseteq (S\times P)\cup (P\times P)\cup (P\times T)$,
as all arcs link either a source with a pool, a pool with another pool, or a pool with a sink.
Let $K$ be a finite set consisting of \emph{flow components} or simply \emph{components}.
For node $i\in N$, define $S_i\subseteq S$ and $T_i\subseteq T$ such that $s\in S_i$ (such that $t\in T_i$)
if and only if $D$ has a path from $s$ to $i$ (from $i$ to $t$), including the possibility that the path consists only of $i$ itself.
Let $P_s={p\in P: s\in S_p}$ and $P_t={p\in P: t\in T_p}$ for all $s\in S$, $t\in T$.
Denote by $N_i^-=\left\{j\in N: (j,i)\in A\right\}$ and $N_i^+=\left\{j\in N: (i,j)\in A\right\}$, respectively,
the upstream and downstream neighbor sets of node $i\in N$.
By the assumption on $A$, $N_s^-=N_t^+=\emptyset$ for every $s\in S$ and $t\in T$.

Vectors (matrices) are written in lowercase (uppercase) bold letters,
and their entries are written in the corresponding lowercase plain letter.
A superscript means that the entry corresponds to some $k\in K$, whereas subscripts are suppplied to identify a node or an arc.
Let the vectors of \emph{unit arc costs} $\boldsymbol{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{A}$ and \emph{node capacities} $\boldsymbol{b}\in\mathbb{Q}_+^{N}$
be associated with $D$.
% At most $b_s$ flow units may enter $D$ at source $s\in S$, and at most $b_t$ units may leave at sink $t\in T$.
Define the matrices of \emph{concentration} $\boldsymbol{Q}\in\mathbb{Q}^{K\times S}$ at the sources, and \emph{concentration bounds}
$\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{Q}^{K\times T}$ at the sinks,
where $q_s^k$ denotes the concentration of component $k$ in the flow entering $D$ at $s$,
and $u_t^k$ is the upper bound on the concentration of component $k$ in the flow leaving $D$ at $t$.
Let $\boldsymbol{q}_s$ ($s\in S$) and $\boldsymbol{u}_t$ ($t\in T$) be the columns of $\boldsymbol{Q}$ and $\boldsymbol{U}$, respectively.

\subsection{Problem definition} \label{sec:problem}

A vector $\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}_+^{A}$ is called a \emph{flow} in $D$ if it satisfies the capacity and flow conservation constraints:
\begin{eqnarray}
           & \sum_{j\in N_i^+}f_{ij}\leq b_i & i\in S\cup P\label{eq:capSP} \\
           & \sum_{j\in N_t^-}f_{jt}\leq b_t & t\in T\label{eq:capT} \\
           & \sum_{j\in N_p^-}f_{jp}-\sum_{j\in N_p^+}f_{pj} = 0~~~~ & p\in P. \label{eq:flowcons}
\end{eqnarray}

\noindent
We refer to the set
$F(D,\boldsymbol{b}) = \left\{\boldsymbol{f}\in\mathbb{R}_+^{A}: \mbox{(\ref{eq:capSP})--(\ref{eq:flowcons}) are satisfied}\right\}$
as the \emph{flow polytope} of $(D,b)$.
For each $\boldsymbol{f}\in F(D,\boldsymbol{b})$,
define $\boldsymbol{W}=\left(\boldsymbol{w}_i\right)_{i\in N}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times N}$ to be a
\emph{concentration matrix} of $\boldsymbol{f}$ if $\boldsymbol{w}_s=\boldsymbol{q}_s$ ($s\in S$) and
\begin{equation} \label{eq:totqual}
  \boldsymbol{w}_i\sum_{j\in N_i^-}f_{ji}=\sum_{j\in N_i^-}\boldsymbol{w}_jf_{ji},\ i\in P\cup T.
\end{equation}
That is, the concentration $\boldsymbol{w}_i=\left(w_i^k\right)_{k\in K}$ of component $k$ in the flow blended at a pool/sink $i$,
is a convex combination of the entering concentrations $\boldsymbol{w}_j$ ($j\in N_i^-$),
with convexity weights $f_{ji}/\sum_{j'\in N_i^-}f_{j'i}$.
Condition \eqref{eq:totqual} on $\boldsymbol{w}$ is referred to as the \emph{linear blending} property.
At sinks $t\in T$ where $\sum_{j\in N_t^-}f_{jt}>0$, the upper concentration bounds $u_t^k$ apply,
and linear blending yields $\boldsymbol{w}_t=\sum_{j\in N_t^-}\boldsymbol{w}_jf_{jt}/\sum_{j\in N_t^-}f_{jt}\leq \boldsymbol{u}_t$.
Any $\boldsymbol{f}\in F\left(D,\boldsymbol{h}\right)$ is said to be a \emph{concentration-feasible} flow in
$D$ if $\sum_{j\in N_t^-}\boldsymbol{w}_jf_{jt}\leq \boldsymbol{u}_t\sum_{j\in N_t^-}f_{jt}$ ($t\in T$)
for some concentration matrix $\boldsymbol{W}$ of $\boldsymbol{f}$.

\begin{problem} \label{prob:pp}
The \textsc{Pooling Problem}: Find a concentration-feasible flow $\boldsymbol{f}\in F(D,\boldsymbol{b})$
minimizing the total costs $\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}$.
\qed
\end{problem}

The minimum cost in Problem~\ref{prob:pp} is denoted $z(D,\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{u})$.
Whenever $A\cap(P\times P)=\emptyset$, Problem~\ref{prob:pp} is referred to as the standard \textsc{Pooling Problem} (SPP),
while the general version is called the \emph{generalized} \textsc{Pooling Problem} (GPP).

\subsection{Formulations and extensions} \label{sec:formext}
In this section, previously studied formulations of the \textsc{Pooling Problem} and its extensions are revised shortly.

\subsubsection{Bilinear formulations of the \textsc{Pooling Problem}} \label{sec:form}
Following the definitons in Section~\ref{sec:problem}, the variables of the P-formulation \cite{haverly78} consist of
flow $f_{ij}$ along arcs $(i,j)\in A$ and the concentrations $w_p^k$ at pools $p\in P$, in addition to \emph{component flow} $z_{ij}^k$.
The model reads (recall that $\boldsymbol{w}_s=\boldsymbol{q}_s$ for all $s\in S$):
\begin{eqnarray}
\min & \sum_{(i,j)\in A}c_{ij}f_{ij} \nonumber \\
     & \boldsymbol{f}\in F(D,\boldsymbol{b}) \nonumber \\
     & \sum_{j\in N_p^+}z_{pj}^k = \sum_{j\in N_p^-}z_{jp}^k  & p\in P, k\in K \label{eq:pcons} \\
     & \sum_{p\in N_t^-}z_{pt}^k \leq u_t^k\sum_{p\in N_t^-}f_{pt} & t\in T, k\in K \label{eq:pqual} \\
     & z_{ij}^k = w_i^kf_{ij} & (i,j)\in A, k\in K \label{eq:pbl}
\end{eqnarray}

In the Q-formulation \cite{bental94} for SPP, strengthened to the PQ-formulation by the addition of valid inequalities \cite{sahinidis05},
and generalized to the MCF-formulation for GPP \cite{alfaki13b}, variables $w_p^k$ and $z_{ij}^k$ are replaced by
$y_p^s\in[0,1]$ and $x_{ij}^s$, respectively ($s\in S_p$, $(i,j)\in A$).
Defining $x_{ij}^s$ as the flow along arc $(i,j)$ supplied from source $s$ ($x_{ij}^s=0$ for $s\in S\setminus S_i$),
and $y_i^s$ as the ratio between $x_{ij}^s$ and $f_{ij}$,
a valid MCF-formulation is obtained by the replacement of \eqref{eq:pcons}--\eqref{eq:pbl} by
(let $y_s^s=1$ and $y_s^{s'}=0$ for $s,s'\in S$, $s\neq s'$):
\begin{eqnarray}
     & \sum_{j\in N_p^+}x_{pj}^s = \sum_{j\in N_p^-}x_{jp}^s  & p\in P, s\in S_p \label{eq:scons} \\
     & \sum_{p\in N_t^-}\sum_{s\in S_p}(q_s^k-u_t^k)x_{pt}^s \leq 0 & t\in T, k\in K \label{eq:squal} \\
     & x_{ij}^s = y_i^sf_{ij} & (i,j)\in A, s\in S_i \label{eq:sbl} \\
     & \sum_{s\in S_p}y_p^s = 1 & p\in P \label{eq:sconv} \\
     & f_{ij} = \sum_{s\in S_i}x_{ij}^s & (i,j)\in A \label{eq:svi1} \\
     & \sum_{j\in N_p^+}x_{pj}^s \leq b_py_p^s & p\in P, s\in S_p \label{eq:svi2} \\
     & y_p^s\geq 0 & p\in P, s\in S_p \label{eq:sy}
\end{eqnarray}

Constraint \eqref{eq:svi1} follows from \eqref{eq:sbl}--\eqref{eq:sconv},
but is added to strengthen the relaxation obtained when corresponding McCormick inequalities \cite{mccormick76} replace \eqref{eq:sbl}.
For the same reason, \eqref{eq:svi2} is added, although it is implied by $\boldsymbol{f}\in F(D,\boldsymbol{b})$
and \eqref{eq:sbl}.

Alternatively, the proportion variables may relate to sinks rather than sources \cite{boland16}.
For $t\in T_j$, let $x_{ij}^t$ denote the flow along arc $(i,j)$ that finally is delivered at sink $t$,
let $x_{ij}^t=0$ for $t\in T\setminus T_j$, and let $y_j^t=x_{ij}^t/f_{ij}$ if $f_{ij}>0$.
A valid formulation is obtained by replacing $S_p$ by $T_p$ in \eqref{eq:scons}, \eqref{eq:sconv}, and \eqref{eq:svi2}--\eqref{eq:sy},
replacing $S_i$ by $T_j$ in \eqref{eq:svi1}, and replacing \eqref{eq:squal}--\eqref{eq:sbl} by
\begin{eqnarray}
     & \sum_{s\in S_t}(q_s^k-u_t^k)\sum_{p\in N_s^+}x_{sp}^t \leq 0 & t\in T, k\in K \label{eq:tqual} \\
     & x_{ij}^t = y_j^tf_{ij} & (i,j)\in A, t\in T_j \label{eq:tbl}
\end{eqnarray}

The two models based on proportion variables can also be combined to create a stronger formulation \cite{boland16}.
To that end, all variables $y_p^v$ ($p\in P$, $v\in S_p\cup T_p$) and $x_{ij}^v$ ($(i,j)\in A$, $v\in S_i\cup T_j$)
and corresponding constraints from both models are pursued.

\subsubsection{Extensions} \label{sec:ext}
Some authors add features that are covered by the definition of Problem~\ref{prob:pp} by simple adjustments of the input data,
and will therefore not be considered as extensions:
\begin{itemize}
\item arcs from sources to sinks (considered in most cited works)
\item arc capacities \cite{boland16}
\item lower bounds on the concentrations at $T$ (considered in \cite{adhya99} and several more recent works).
\end{itemize}

Other additions require more constraints in the model.
Noteworthy among these are concentration bounds at the pools \cite{ruiz13} and lower bounds on the flow at the nodes.
\citet{grothey23} consider a variant where the demands at $T$ are fixed, i.e.\ the inequalities \eqref{eq:capT} are replaced by equalities
(also assumed in \cite{ruiz13}).
While providing no complexity results, \cite{grothey23} states that the fixed-demand SPP tends to `have large numbers of local optima'.
Next, we show that when the demand is fixed at all sinks, the instances with only one pool are solvable in polynomial time,
contrasting \cite[Corollary 1]{alfaki13a}.
\begin{proposition} \label{prop:grothey1}
If all inequalities \eqref{eq:capT} are replaced by equalities, then Problem~\ref{prob:pp} is an LP in instances where $|P|=1$. 
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Recall that $D$ has no arcs from $S$ to $T$, which means that exactly $d=\sum_{t\in T}b_t$
flow units pass through the pool (denoted $p$).
Since also $N_t^-=\{p\}$ for all $t\in T$, equation \eqref{eq:totqual} reads
$\boldsymbol{w}_pd = \sum_{s\in S}\boldsymbol{q}_sf_{sp}$ and $\boldsymbol{w}_t=\boldsymbol{w}_p$.
It follows that $\boldsymbol{f}$ is a concentration-feasible flow satisfying the fixed demand constraints
if and only if $\boldsymbol{f}\in F(D,\boldsymbol{b})$, $f_{pt}=b_t$ ($t\in T$), and
$\sum_{s\in S}q_s^kf_{sp}\leq d\min_{t\in T}u_t^k$ for all $k\in K$.
\end{proof}

With two pools, also the fixed-demand variant becomes NP-hard:
\begin{proposition} \label{prop:grothey2}
If all inequalities \eqref{eq:capT} are replaced by equalities, then Problem~\ref{prob:pp} is NP-hard, even for instances where $|P|=2$. 
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The proof resembles the reduction from \textsc{Independent Vertex Set} considered in the proofs of \cite[Proposition 1]{alfaki13a},
\cite[Proposition 2.1]{gupte17}, and \cite[Proposition 3]{dey15}.

Let $G=(V,E)$ be a graph with vertex set $V$ and edge set $E$.
Let $S=\{s_v: v\in V\}\cup\{\bar{s}\}$, $T=\{t_v: v\in V\}$, and $K=\{k_v: v\in V\}$, $P=\{p,\bar{p}\}$,
and $A=\{(s_v,p), (p,t_v), (\bar{s},\bar{p}), (\bar{p},t_v): v\in V\}$.
Hence there is a bijection between $V$ and each of the sets $S\setminus\{\bar{s}\}$, $T$, and $K$,
and for any $k\in K$, we denote by $v(k)$ the corresponding vertex of $G$.
An analogous notation is adopted for the other sets.
For nodes indexed $i\in\{s_v, t_v: v\in V\}$, the capacity is $b_i=1$, whereas $b_i=|V|$ for $i\in\{\bar{s},p,\bar{p}\}$.
Let the arc costs be $c_{ij}=0$ for $i\in S\cup\{p\}$, and $c_{ij}=1$ for $i=\bar{p}$.
Finally, the concentration of component $k$ at source $s\neq \bar{s}$ is $q_s^k=1$ if $v(s)=v(k)$, and $q_s^k=0$ otherwise,
while $\boldsymbol{q}_{\bar{s}}=\boldsymbol{0}$.
Bounds on the concentrations are given as $u_t^k=0$ if $\{v(t),v(k)\}\in E$, and $u_t^k=1$ if $\{v(t),v(k)\}\notin E$.
That is, flow from source $s\neq\bar{s}$ is compatible with the bounds at sink $t$ if and only if $\{v(s),v(t)\}\notin E$,
whereas flow can be sent from $\bar{s}$ to any sink.
See Fig.~\ref{fig:nphard} for an illustration.

Let $V'\subseteq V$ be an independent vertex set in $G$, and consider the flow $\boldsymbol{f}$ where
$f_{sp}=1$ if $v(s)\in V'$ and $f_{sp}=0$ if $v(s)\notin V'$,
$f_{pt}=1$ if $v(t)\in V'$ and $f_{pt}=0$ if $v(t)\notin V'$,
$f_{\bar{s}\bar{p}}=|T|-|V'|$, and $f_{\bar{p}t}=1-f_{pt}$ ($t\in T$).
It follows that $\boldsymbol{f}\in F(D,\boldsymbol{b})$ and $\sum_{p\in N_t^-}f_{pt}=b_t$ for all $t\in T$.
Also, if $f_{sp}=f_{pt}=1$, then $\{v(s),v(t)\}\notin E$, and $q_s\leq u_t$ for all $k\in K$ shows that
$\boldsymbol{f}$ satisfies the concentration bounds at all $t_v$ where $v\in V'$.
For $v\in V\setminus V'$, the concentration of all components is zero at $t_v$.
It follows that the \textsc{Pooling Problem} instance has a feasible solution with cost $|T|-|V'|$.
Thus, $z(D,\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{u})\leq |T|-\alpha(G)$,
where $\alpha(G)$ denotes the independence number of $G$.

To prove $z(D,\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{b},\boldsymbol{q},\boldsymbol{u})\geq |T|-\alpha(G)$,
assume $\boldsymbol{f}$ is optimal.
Consider the vertices of $G$ corresponding to active sources $V_S = \left\{v\in V: f_{s_vp}>0\right\}$ and active sinks
$V_T = \left\{v\in V: f_{pt_v}>0\right\}$, respectively.
Because $\boldsymbol{f}$ is optimal, it can be assumed that $|V_S|=|V_T|$ and $f_{sp}=f_{pt}=1$ if $v(s)\in V_S$ and $v(t)\in V_T$
(\textcolor{red}{Should we include this proof? To see this, assume on the contrary that $|V_T|>|V_S|$.
Then, without loss of optimality, the flow along arc $(p,\tau)$, where $\tau\in\arg\min\{f_{pt}: v(t)\in V_T\}$,
can be reallocated to other arcs $(p,t)$ where $v(t)\in V_T$.
Hence, $|V_T|\leq|V_S|$ can be assumed, and the assumption $|V_S|\leq|V_T|$ is justified analogously.
To prove $f_{sp}=f_{pt}=1$ for all $s,t$ where $v(s)\in V_S$ and $v(t)\in V_T$, assume $f_{pt}<1$ and $v(t)\in V_T$.
Conservation of flow at $p$ and $|V_S|=|V_T|$ imply that $f_{sp}<1$ for some $s\in S$ where $v(s)\in V_S$.
Since the fixed demand at $t$ is 1, $f_{\bar{p}t}>0$.
By reducing the flow along the path $(\bar{s},\bar{p},t)$ by an amount $\delta\leq f_{\bar{p}t}$, 
and increasing the flow by $\delta$ units along the path $(s,p,t)$,
another feasible solution with lower cost than $\boldsymbol{f}$ is found, contradicting optimality.
})
It follows from the fixed demand at all $t\in T$ that $f_{\bar{p}t}=1-f_{pt}$,
yielding $\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{f}=\sum_{t\in T:v(t)\in V\setminus V_T}f_{\bar{p}t}=|T|-|V_T|$

Let $W=V_S\cap V_T$.
To prove $V_S=V_T=W$, assume there exists a $w\in V_S\setminus V_T$.
Since $|V_S|=|V_T|$, there also exists some $w'\in V_T\setminus V_S$.
Hence, $f_{s_{w'}p}=f_{pt_w}=0$, whereas $f_{\bar{p}t_w}=1$.
Because $\{w',w\}\notin E$, and $\{w',v\}\notin E$ for all $v\in V_T$,
flow can be augmented by one unit along the path $(s_{w'},p,t_w)$ while respecting the concentration bounds
at all sinks $t_v$ ($v\in V_T\cup\{w\}$).
To maintain the demand at $t_w$, the flow is reduced by one unit along $(\bar{s},\bar{p},t_w)$.
As the resulting flow is feasible with a cost one unit less than the cost of $\boldsymbol{f}$,
we have arrived at a contradiction that proves $V_S\setminus V_T=\emptyset$.
That $V_T\setminus V_S=\emptyset$ is proved analogously, and thus, $V_S=V_T=W$.

From the concentration bounds at $t_v$, it follows that $v\in V_T=W$ and $v'\in V_S=W$ only if $\{v',v\}\notin E$.
Hence, $W$ is independent.
The proof is complete by observing that $\boldsymbol{c}\cdot\boldsymbol{f} = |T|-|W|\geq|T|-\alpha(G)$.
\end{proof}
